### 📅 Scientific Anniversaries
* **🧠 July 6, 1924: Listening to the Brain's Electricity**
  German psychiatrist Hans Berger recorded the first-ever human electroencephalogram, quietly proving that thought itself leaves a measurable electrical signature. He was chasing a personal obsession with telepathy and found something arguably more useful instead: a way to "see" the brain thinking without ever opening the skull. Every sleep study and epilepsy diagnosis since owes him a debt.
* **❄️ July 10, 1908: Colder Than Anyone Thought Possible**
  Dutch physicist Heike Kamerlingh Onnes finally liquefied helium at his lab in Leiden, dropping it to 4.2 kelvin, about -269°C. Helium was the last gas standing between physicists and absolute zero, and cracking it didn't just win Onnes a Nobel Prize; it handed him the exact conditions needed to stumble onto superconductivity three years later, a phenomenon still doing the heavy lifting inside every MRI machine and quantum computer built today.
* **🛰️ July 10, 1962: The World Gets a Nervous System**
  Bell Labs and NASA launched Telstar 1, the first active communications satellite, capable of receiving a signal, amplifying it, and beaming it straight back down to another continent. Live transatlantic television and telephone calls suddenly became possible. It burned out after a few months from radiation damage, but it proved that orbit itself could work as global infrastructure, a bet every modern satellite constellation is still cashing in on.

### 🎂 Birthdays & Legacies
* **🔬 July 7: [Camillo Golgi](https://scientific-chronicles.vercel.app/date/07-07.html#camillo-golgi.html) (b. 1843)**
  The Italian physician who, almost by accident, invented the "black reaction," a silver nitrate stain that turned individual nerve cells fully black under the microscope for the first time. Before Golgi, the nervous system looked like a tangled mess. After him, it was a map of distinct, nameable cells. He shared the 1906 Nobel Prize with his fiercest scientific rival, Santiago Ramón y Cajal, despite the two of them disagreeing to the end about how neurons actually connect.
* **🕳️ July 9: [John Wheeler](https://scientific-chronicles.vercel.app/date/07-09.html#john-wheeler.html) (b. 1911)**
  A serious physicist who did serious work in general relativity and quantum gravity, and mentored a roster of students including Richard Feynman. But his most lasting contribution to science might be linguistic: in 1967 he popularized the term "black hole," and separately coined "wormhole," giving the rest of us a way to talk about objects our intuition can barely process. Sometimes the biggest contribution to physics is just naming the thing well enough that everyone else can join the conversation.
* **⚡ July 10: [Nikola Tesla](https://scientific-chronicles.vercel.app/date/07-10.html#nikola-tesla.html) (b. 1856)**
  The Serbian-American inventor whose patents on alternating current turned electricity from a laboratory curiosity into a power grid that could reach an entire continent. Every wall socket you have ever used is downstream of the AC-versus-DC fight he picked and effectively won.

### 🤓 The "Did You Know?" Corner
**The Day That Broke the Calendar** 📅
We already covered helium and Telstar above, but July 10th wasn't finished. That same date also happens to be Nikola Tesla's birthday (1856), the opening day of the Scopes "Monkey" Trial in Dayton, Tennessee (1925), where a substitute teacher was put on trial for teaching evolution in a case that became a national media circus over science versus scripture, and, just to round things out, it's also the day Volvo engineer Nils Bohlin filed the patent for the three-point seatbelt in 1962, the same year as Telstar's launch. Volvo famously left that patent open for every other manufacturer to use for free. Five completely unrelated, era-defining moments in physics, biology, law, and road safety, all sharing one square on the calendar, spread across more than a century. History doesn't file things chronologically so much as it just piles them up.

### 🚀 Closing Thoughts
The throughline this week isn't really electricity, or cold, or orbits. It's the act of making the invisible legible. **Berger** turned thought into a squiggly line on paper. **Golgi** turned a tangle of nerve fibers into a stainable, countable structure. **Onnes** turned a gas nobody could pin down into a liquid, and then into a doorway onto a whole new state of matter. **Wheeler** turned a mathematical singularity nobody could picture into a two-word phrase a kid can say out loud. And **Tesla** turned a force you can't see or touch into something that lights up a room at the flip of a switch.

None of that happens by accident, even when the calendar makes it look that way. Whatever you're staring at this week that still feels formless, the first move is usually the same one: find a way to make it visible.
