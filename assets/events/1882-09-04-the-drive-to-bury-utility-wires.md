Picture a late 19th-century city street, not just bustling with horse-drawn carriages, but also tangled in a dizzying, dangerous mess of overhead wires – for telegraphs, telephones, and soon, electric power. It was an aesthetic nightmare and a literal hazard! After New York's Pearl Street Station lit up in 1882 (with Thomas Edison himself pushing for some undergrounding), the sheer volume of wires became undeniable. Public outrage over deadly fallen lines and urban blight sparked a major movement to bury them. This wasn't a snap decision; it involved immense engineering challenges – insulation, waterproofing, heat dissipation, and costly excavation. But the eventual, gradual transition to underground utilities dramatically cleaned up cityscapes, made them safer, and showcased a monumental shift in urban planning and electrical engineering. Sometimes, the best innovations are the ones you can't see!
