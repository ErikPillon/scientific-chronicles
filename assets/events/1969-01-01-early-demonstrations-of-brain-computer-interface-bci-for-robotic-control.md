The idea of controlling machines with thought sounds like science fiction, but it became a scientific reality through Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs). While early BCI experiments in animals in the late 1960s showed the potential, the real "wow" factor for *robotic control* truly took off in the late 1990s and early 2000s. In 1998, a human patient with locked-in syndrome successfully used a BCI to control a computer cursor. Building on this, by the mid-2000s, researchers like John P. Donoghue and Miguel Nicolelis demonstrated that paralyzed individuals could use implanted electrode arrays to directly control sophisticated robotic arms, picking up objects, and even drinking coffee. This wasn't just a neat trick; it was a monumental leap, offering profound hope for restoring independence to those with severe motor impairments, and opening new frontiers in human-machine interaction.
