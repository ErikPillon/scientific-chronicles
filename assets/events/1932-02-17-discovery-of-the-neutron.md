In the wild world of 1930s physics, things were getting weird. Scientists knew about protons and electrons, but the atomic nucleus was still a bit of a mystery. Then, in 1930, Walther Bothe and Herbert Becker noticed something odd: when they bombarded beryllium with alpha particles (helium nuclei), a highly penetrating, uncharged radiation popped out. Two years later, Irène and Frédéric Joliot-Curie picked up on this, observing that this 'radiation' could knock protons out of paraffin. They thought it was gamma rays – super-energetic light. Enter James Chadwick, a British physicist with a gut feeling something was off. He wasn't convinced by the gamma ray theory and hypothesized that this mysterious radiation must be composed of neutral particles with a mass similar to protons. With meticulous experiments at Cambridge, he confirmed his hypothesis, announcing the discovery of the 'neutron' in February 1932. Boom! The puzzle pieces of the atom finally clicked into place, paving the way for nuclear fission and the atomic age. Who knew shooting stuff at beryllium could be so groundbreaking?
