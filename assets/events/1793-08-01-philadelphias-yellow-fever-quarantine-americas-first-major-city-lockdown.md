Before masks and social distancing became buzzwords, American cities were grappling with deadly epidemics the hard way. In 1793, Philadelphia, then the nation's capital and largest city, was ravaged by a terrifying yellow fever outbreak. With panic spreading faster than the disease, authorities were forced to take drastic action. While less sophisticated than modern quarantines, a 'cordon sanitaire' was established around the city, and a lazaretto (quarantine hospital) was set up downriver to isolate incoming ships and sick individuals. Thousands fled, businesses shut down, and the city essentially ground to a halt. This wasn't just a public health crisis; it was a political and social nightmare, prompting fierce debates over how to contain disease without crushing civil liberties. This harrowing experience, with figures like Dr. Benjamin Rush and businessman Stephen Girard working tirelessly, laid crucial groundwork for future public health policies in the United States, proving that sometimes, locking down is the only way to beat back an unseen enemy.
