Imagine a bustling New York City where clean water was a luxury, fires were catastrophic, and disease lurked in every polluted well. That was NYC before 1842. Then came the Croton Aqueduct, a monumental feat of civil engineering designed by chief engineer John B. Jervis. On October 14, 1842, a celebratory parade marked the moment water from the Croton River, 41 miles away, finally flowed into the city's reservoirs. This wasn't just about quenching thirst; it dramatically improved sanitation, reduced waterborne illnesses, and provided ample pressure for fighting the city's frequent infernos. It was a game-changer, literally laying the foundation for modern urban infrastructure and proving that with enough grit (and good engineering), you can move mountains – or at least a river's worth of water.
