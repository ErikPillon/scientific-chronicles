Before Netflix, before a million streaming apps, and even before widespread satellite dishes, there was cable TV – a technological marvel that fundamentally changed how we consumed media. Its "birth" wasn't a single, dramatic "eureka!" moment, but rather a gradual, inventive evolution primarily driven by a very practical need: getting better television reception in rural or mountainous areas where over-the-air signals were weak or non-existent. In the late 1940s, resourceful pioneers like John Walson, an appliance store owner in Mahanoy City, Pennsylvania, and Ed Parsons in Astoria, Oregon, started setting up what were called community antenna television (CATV) systems. They'd erect a tall antenna on a hill, capture distant broadcast signals, and then string wires from that central antenna directly to multiple homes, for a fee. Suddenly, remote viewers could enjoy clear pictures and a wider selection of channels than just the few terrestrial broadcasters could offer. This ingenious, albeit humble, beginning laid the essential groundwork for the sprawling cable networks and content empires of today, ushering in an era of specialized programming and a truly global viewing experience, proving that sometimes, the biggest media revolutions start with simply needing a better signal.
