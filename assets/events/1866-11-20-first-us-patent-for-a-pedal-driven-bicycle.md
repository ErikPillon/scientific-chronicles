The journey from Karl Drais's 'running machine' to the modern bicycle was a bumpy, often litigious, ride. While pedal-driven velocipedes appeared in France in the 1860s, credit for the first U.S. patent for a pedal-driven bicycle often goes to Pierre Lallement. A French mechanic who had worked with the Michaux brothers (who were mass-producing velocipedes), Lallement moved to the U.S. and filed his patent application in 1866, which was granted on November 20, 1866. His design featured cranks and pedals attached to the front wheel, making it a true ancestor of the modern bike, even if it was a bone-shaking 'boneshaker' to ride! This patent marked a crucial legal and commercial milestone, laying claim to a technology that would soon sweep the world. While various claims and counter-claims exist regarding who invented what first in those frenetic early years, Lallement's U.S. patent solidified a significant piece of intellectual property in the budding bicycle industry, setting the stage for the innovations that followed.
