Before FORTRAN, programming computers was a painstaking, error-prone ordeal, requiring intimate knowledge of machine code. Then, in 1957, a team at IBM led by John Backus unveiled FORTRAN (FORmula TRANslation), and everything changed. This wasn't just another programming language; it was the first widely adopted high-level language that allowed programmers to use human-readable syntax (like mathematical formulas) instead of cryptic machine instructions. Suddenly, scientists and engineers could write complex programs much faster and with fewer errors, dramatically accelerating research in fields from aerospace to nuclear physics. FORTRAN democratized computing, making it practical for problem-solving beyond the realm of specialized hardware experts. It's a testament to its robust design that versions of FORTRAN are still in use today, proving its monumental impact on the digital world.
