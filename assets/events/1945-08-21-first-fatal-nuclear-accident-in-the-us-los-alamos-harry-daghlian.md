The Manhattan Project was a race against time, but it came with immense risks. On August 21, 1945, just days after the atomic bombings of Japan, physicist Harry Daghlian was performing a critical mass experiment at Los Alamos. He was "tickling the dragon's tail," slowly bringing subcritical masses of fissile material together. During an accidental drop of a tungsten carbide brick onto a plutonium core, the assembly briefly went supercritical, resulting in a burst of neutron radiation. Daghlian immediately received a lethal dose, dying 25 days later from acute radiation poisoning. His tragic death underscored the extreme dangers of handling nuclear materials and led to increased safety protocols, a grim reminder of the costs of pioneering science.
