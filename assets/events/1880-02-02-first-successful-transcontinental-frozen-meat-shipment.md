Before the late 19th century, getting fresh meat from Australia to Europe was a culinary pipe dream – unless you liked your beef jerky-style. But thanks to burgeoning refrigeration technology, primarily pioneered by James Harrison and Eugene Dominique Nicolle, the game changed. In 1879, the steamship *Strathleven*, equipped with a refrigeration system, loaded a precious cargo of frozen beef and mutton in Sydney and Melbourne, bound for London. After a 60-day voyage, arriving on February 2, 1880, the meat was found to be perfectly preserved, fetching good prices. This wasn't just a successful delivery; it was a monumental leap in global trade, proving that perishable goods could travel vast distances. It kickstarted Australia and New Zealand's frozen meat export industries, fed a growing British population, and shrunk the world, one perfectly chilled lamb chop at a time.
