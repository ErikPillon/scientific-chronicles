In August 1877, while peering through the "Great Equatorial" telescope at the U.S. Naval Observatory, American astronomer Asaph Hall made a cosmic double-take. Just days after discovering Phobos, he found its even tinier sibling, Deimos, orbiting Mars. Imagine the thrill of finding a whole new celestial body, even if it was just a lumpy, 12-kilometer-wide rock! Hall, after some initial doubts, confirmed his discovery, naming it after the Greek personification of dread, one of the mythological attendants of Ares (Mars). This finding cemented our understanding of Mars's peculiar satellite system and added another couple of celestial nuggets to our astronomical inventory.
