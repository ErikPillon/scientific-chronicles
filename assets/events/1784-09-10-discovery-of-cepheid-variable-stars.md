It all started in 1784 when English astronomer Edward Pigott noticed a star in the constellation Cepheus that brightened and dimmed rhythmically – the first Cepheid variable. These celestial beacons became far more famous thanks to Henrietta Swan Leavitt in the early 20th century. Working at Harvard, she discovered a direct relationship between a Cepheid's pulsation period and its intrinsic luminosity. This Period-Luminosity relationship turned Cepheids into 'standard candles,' allowing astronomers to measure distances to galaxies far, far away, basically giving us a cosmic tape measure. Talk about a glow-up for a blinking star!
