On March 1, 1954, a routine (if 'routine' can be used for nuclear bombs) test took a terrifying turn. The United States detonated the 'Castle Bravo' hydrogen bomb at Bikini Atoll in the Marshall Islands. Intended to be a 6-megaton blast, the experimental device unexpectedly yielded 15 megatons – more than 1,000 times the power of the Hiroshima bomb – making it the most powerful nuclear weapon ever detonated by the U.S. and the largest man-made explosion in history. The sheer force vaporized a huge section of the coral reef, creating a crater over a mile wide. More chillingly, the larger-than-expected yield, combined with unfavorable winds, sent a massive cloud of highly radioactive fallout drifting eastward. It contaminated the crew of the Japanese fishing boat, the Daigo Fukuryū Maru ('Lucky Dragon No. 5'), and severely impacted several inhabited atolls, forcing evacuations and causing long-term health effects. Castle Bravo was a stark, terrifying demonstration of the destructive power of thermonuclear weapons and a major international incident, forever cementing the Bikini Atoll as a symbol of the atomic age's immense power and its devastating consequences.
