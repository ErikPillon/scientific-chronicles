Picture this: it's the 1950s, and scientists are trying to figure out why cells, when infected by one virus, sometimes become resistant to other viruses. It’s like a biological 'no vacancy' sign! In 1957, Scottish virologist Alick Isaacs and Swiss microbiologist Jean Lindenmann made the breakthrough, discovering a substance produced by infected cells that 'interfered' with viral replication. They appropriately named it 'interferon.' This wasn't just a quirky biological observation; it unveiled a fundamental part of our innate immune system, a built-in defense mechanism that cells use to warn their neighbors about unwelcome viral guests. From pioneering our understanding of viral defense to becoming a crucial tool in treating diseases like hepatitis and certain cancers, interferon proved that sometimes, the best defense is a good offense – chemically speaking!
