Picture this: It's the early 1930s, and Bell Labs engineer Karl Jansky is trying to pinpoint sources of static that interfere with shortwave radio communications. He builds a rather peculiar rotating antenna, affectionately nicknamed 'Jansky's merry-go-round,' and tracks down three types of static. Two were obvious — local thunderstorms and distant ones. But the third? A faint, persistent hiss that peaked every 23 hours and 56 minutes, the sidereal day. Jansky realized it wasn't from Earth; it was coming from space, specifically the constellation Sagittarius, the direction of the Milky Way's core. This accidental cosmic eavesdropping in 1931, published in 1933, birthed the entire field of radio astronomy, proving the universe sings in more ways than just visible light. Who knew static could be so profound?
