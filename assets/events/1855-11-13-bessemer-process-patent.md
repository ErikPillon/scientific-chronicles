Before Henry Bessemer came along, steel was a luxurious pain to make – expensive, time-consuming, and only for the fanciest of projects. Cast iron, its cheaper cousin, was brittle. But Bessemer, a prolific British inventor, had a eureka moment in the mid-1850s. While trying to improve artillery, he realized that blowing air through molten pig iron would oxidize and remove impurities like silicon, manganese, and carbon, transforming it into steel. His first patent for this process, filed in 1855, was a huge deal, though he refined it further with subsequent patents, notably in 1856. The Bessemer process dramatically reduced the cost and time of steel production, making it widely available for everything from railways and bridges to skyscrapers and ships. It wasn't without its initial hiccups and legal battles, but Bessemer's ingenuity kickstarted the second Industrial Revolution, shaping the modern world in ways few other inventions ever have. Suddenly, strong, affordable steel was everywhere, all thanks to a blast of air.
