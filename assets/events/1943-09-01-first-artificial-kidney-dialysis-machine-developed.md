During the dark days of World War II in occupied Netherlands, physician Willem Johan Kolff was a man on a mission. Faced with a desperate shortage of resources and patients dying from kidney failure, Kolff ingeniously cobbled together the world's first artificial kidney. Using everyday materials like a washing machine, cellophane tubing, and orange juice cans, he built a rotating drum machine that could filter a patient's blood. His initial attempts were, let's say, not always successful, but by 1945, he saved a patient from uremia. This groundbreaking device, born out of necessity and brilliant improvisation, transformed kidney failure from an almost certain death sentence into a treatable condition, laying the foundation for modern dialysis.
