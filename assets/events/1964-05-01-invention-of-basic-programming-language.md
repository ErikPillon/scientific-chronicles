Before user-friendly interfaces, coding was a mystical art practiced by a few high priests of computing. Then came BASIC (Beginner's All-purpose Symbolic Instruction Code), born in 1964 at Dartmouth College thanks to John G. Kemeny and Thomas E. Kurtz. Their big idea? To create a language simple enough for anyone, not just rocket scientists, to learn and use. BASIC was designed to be interactive, allowing users to type commands directly into a terminal and see results immediately. It didn't just teach students about computers; it democratized access, opening the door for a generation of hobbyists and future tech giants. While it might seem clunky now, with its 'GOTO' statements and line numbers, BASIC was the gateway drug for countless programmers, becoming synonymous with the early personal computer era. It sparked creativity, enabling everything from simple games to business applications, proving that complex technology could be made delightfully approachable.
