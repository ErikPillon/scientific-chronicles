Long before Deep Blue famously outwitted Garry Kasparov, the idea of computers playing chess was a gleam in the eye of pioneering AI researchers. In the 1950s, brilliant minds like Claude Shannon and Alan Turing were already sketching out algorithms for chess programs, recognizing the game's perfect blend of logic and complexity as an ideal test for machine intelligence. Turing even wrote a program, "Turochamp," in 1950, though he had to manually execute its moves! These early attempts, while rudimentary by today's standards, laid the essential groundwork for artificial intelligence, pushing the boundaries of what machines could "think" and "decide." It wasn't just about winning a game; it was about understanding how intelligent decisions could be formalized and simulated, opening up a whole new field of study. From those initial, clunky moves, a digital grandmaster eventually emerged.
