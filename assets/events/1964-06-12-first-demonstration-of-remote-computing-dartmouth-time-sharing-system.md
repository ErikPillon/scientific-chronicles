Before the internet made remote access commonplace, computers were solitary beasts, serving one user at a time. But on June 12, 1964, John Kemeny and Thomas Kurtz at Dartmouth College unveiled a game-changing innovation: the Dartmouth Time-Sharing System (DTSS). This wasn't just a clever trick; it allowed multiple students and researchers to simultaneously connect to and use a single powerful mainframe computer from different terminals. Instead of waiting hours for a batch job, users could interact with the computer in real-time, fostering a new era of interactive computing. It democratized access to computing power, paving the way for modern operating systems, networks, and the very concept of remote access that underpins our connected world today.
