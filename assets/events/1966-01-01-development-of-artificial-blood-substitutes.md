Imagine a world where blood transfusions aren't limited by donor availability or compatibility issues. That's the holy grail of "artificial blood transfusion," a journey that really heated up in the mid-20th century. While early attempts at blood substitutes go back centuries (think milk, or even wine!), modern artificial blood research focuses on developing synthetic oxygen carriers. A major breakthrough came in 1966 when Leland Clark successfully kept a mouse alive submerged in oxygenated perfluorocarbon liquid – effectively "liquid breathing." This sparked intense research into perfluorocarbons and later, hemoglobin-based oxygen carriers (HBOCs). The goal wasn't just a sci-fi novelty; it was to create a universal blood substitute that could be stored for long periods, transport oxygen efficiently, and be given to anyone, regardless of blood type. It's a complex puzzle of biochemistry and medicine, showing how scientists tirelessly work to overcome biological limitations, proving that sometimes, you just have to invent a better way to keep the oxygen flowing.
