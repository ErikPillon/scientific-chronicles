Imagine announcing a groundbreaking discovery, only to realize you left out a crucial detail! That's what happened in 1991 when astronomers led by Andrew Lyne published in Nature the discovery of a planet orbiting the pulsar PSR 1829–10, claiming it was the first confirmed exoplanet. The astronomical community was buzzing! However, during a conference a few months later, Lyne dramatically revealed that a coding error in their software, linked to the Earth's orbital motion, had created a false signal. Whoops! While an embarrassing moment, it was a valuable lesson in the meticulous cross-checking required for such monumental discoveries. The real first exoplanet around a pulsar was found later that year. Better to be wrong and learn than never to try, right?
