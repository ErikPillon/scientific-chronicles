On September 13, 1899, New York City witnessed a grim milestone: the first recorded automobile fatality in the United States. Henry H. Bliss, a real estate broker, was stepping off a streetcar at West 74th Street and Central Park West when an electric taxicab veered and struck him. Bliss suffered head and chest injuries, dying the next morning. The driver, Arthur Smith, was arrested on a charge of manslaughter but was later acquitted. This wasn't just a statistic; it was a stark, early warning that the horseless carriage, while a marvel of engineering, carried inherent dangers. As automobiles became more common, the need for traffic laws, safety measures, and public awareness about road safety grew exponentially. Bliss's tragic death marked the beginning of a sobering conversation about the responsibilities that came with the burgeoning automotive age, reminding everyone that progress often comes with unforeseen costs.
