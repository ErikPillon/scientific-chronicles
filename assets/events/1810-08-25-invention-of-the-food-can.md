Before the food can, keeping grub fresh for long journeys or off-season was a constant headache – salting, smoking, and drying could only do so much. The hero of this story is Nicolas Appert, a French confectioner who, in 1800, discovered that food cooked in sealed glass bottles wouldn't spoil. The French government, desperate to feed its armies, offered a prize, and Appert won it! But glass was fragile. Enter Peter Durand, a British merchant, who in 1810 patented the idea of using tin-coated iron cans for preservation, directly inspired by Appert's method. These early cans were thick, heavy, and often required a hammer and chisel to open (can openers wouldn't appear until much later!). But despite their quirks, they were revolutionary. Suddenly, food could be stored for years, enabling longer sea voyages, military campaigns, and offering fresh (or at least, non-rotten) provisions year-round. The food can transformed human logistics, nutrition, and even global diets, proving that sometimes, the biggest innovations come in a very small, sealed package.
