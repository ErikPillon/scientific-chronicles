In April 1972, Apollo 16 blasted off, taking astronauts John Young, Ken Mattingly, and Charles Duke on a geological expedition to the moon's rugged Descartes Highlands. Young and Duke spent a whopping 71 hours on the lunar surface, zipping around in their lunar rover, collecting rock samples and generally being space-faring trailblazers. Mattingly, meanwhile, orbited above, snapping pictures and keeping the mothership company. This mission was a treasure trove for scientists, bringing back samples that helped us understand the moon's volcanic past – or lack thereof – challenging previous assumptions. It was less about majestic plains and more about craggy highlands, proving the moon still had plenty of surprises up its dusty sleeve and pushing the boundaries of lunar geology.
