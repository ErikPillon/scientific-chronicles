The terrifying specter of polio, especially its paralytic form, once gripped the world. When the virus attacked the respiratory muscles, patients literally couldn't breathe on their own. Enter the iron lung! Invented by Philip Drinker and Louis Agassiz Shaw at Harvard in 1928, and later improved by John Haven Emerson, this imposing metal cylinder encased a patient's body, creating negative pressure around them, which mechanically forced air into their lungs. The first successful use on a human patient was on October 12, 1928. While it looked like something out of a sci-fi movie, the iron lung saved countless lives during polio epidemics, becoming a symbol of both medical innovation and the sheer will to survive. Although largely replaced by more modern ventilators and, thankfully, the polio vaccine, the iron lung stands as a powerful testament to ingenuity in the face of a devastating disease.
