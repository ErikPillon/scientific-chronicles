Imagine the sun, nearly eclipsed, when suddenly, a string of dazzling, jewel-like points of light appear along the moon's edge, like a cosmic pearl necklace. That's 'Baily's Beads,' a breathtaking phenomenon named after British astronomer Francis Baily, who meticulously described them during an annular solar eclipse on May 15, 1836. These 'beads' aren't magic; they're sunlight streaming through the valleys and irregularities on the lunar surface just before totality (or just after). As the moon moves, these last rays of sunlight peek through, creating a fleeting, shimmering spectacle that lasts for mere seconds. It's a reminder that even our seemingly smooth celestial neighbors have rugged topography, and that sometimes, the most dramatic astronomical events offer the most delicate visual details. Baily's careful observation helped astronomers better understand the moon's limb profile and remains one of the most anticipated sights for eclipse chasers worldwide.
