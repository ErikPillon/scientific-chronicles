After World War II, Britain's roads were, to put it mildly, a bit of a mess. Congestion was rampant, and the notion of fast, uninterrupted travel seemed like a distant dream. Enter the motorway. On December 5, 1958, a rather chilly day, Prime Minister Harold Macmillan officially opened Britain's very first motorway: the Preston Bypass, a modest eight-mile stretch of what would become the mighty M6. Designed by the visionary Lancashire County Surveyor James Drake, this pioneering piece of asphalt was a bold experiment in modern engineering. It promised to whisk drivers around the notorious bottlenecks of Preston, showcasing a future where traffic flowed freely, unhindered by roundabouts or busy town centres. While small, it was a giant leap for British transport, revolutionising how people and goods moved across the country and setting the blueprint for the vast motorway network we know (and sometimes curse) today. What a breath of fresh air for the tired motorist!
