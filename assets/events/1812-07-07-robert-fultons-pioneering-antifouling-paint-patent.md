Ever wondered why a ship doesn't turn into a mobile coral reef after a few months at sea? Thank antifouling paint! And way back in 1812, none other than steamboat visionary Robert Fulton secured the very first U.S. patent for such a concoction. On July 7, 1812, Fulton, already a celebrated engineer and inventor, patented a method to protect ship hulls from the pesky growth of barnacles, algae, and other marine organisms. These biological hitchhikers could slow vessels down, increase fuel consumption, and damage the hull. Fulton’s patent wasn't just a minor improvement; it was a crucial step in maintaining naval efficiency and maritime commerce. This early innovation showcased Fulton's diverse genius, extending beyond engines and paddlewheels to the nitty-gritty of keeping a boat afloat and efficient. It proved that even the most revolutionary minds appreciate the importance of a clean bottom.
