Before 1582, much of Europe was still running on the Julian calendar, which, while revolutionary for its time, had a slight flaw: it made the year just a smidge too long. Over centuries, this tiny discrepancy meant that seasonal events, particularly the all-important Easter, were slowly drifting from their intended dates. Enter Pope Gregory XIII, who, not one to let a liturgical holiday wander, commissioned a major overhaul. Led by astronomer Aloysius Lilius and mathematician Christopher Clavius, the Gregorian calendar was born. It literally skipped ten days (poof! October 4th went straight to October 15th for those who adopted it immediately) to realign with the seasons and introduced a more accurate leap year rule. While many Catholic countries embraced it right away, others clung to the old ways, leading to centuries of chronological confusion. It's the calendar we still use today, proving that sometimes, even a Pope can get the math spot on.
