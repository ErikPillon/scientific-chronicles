Before 1913, building a car was a bit like a complicated culinary show: each vehicle was assembled in one spot, with teams of skilled workers scurrying around it. It was slow, expensive, and definitely not suitable for making cars 'for the great multitude.' Then came Henry Ford's eureka moment – the moving assembly line. On October 7, 1913, at his Highland Park plant, Ford's team started pushing car chassis along a line, with workers adding specific parts as it passed by. This wasn't entirely new; slaughterhouses had reverse assembly lines for years. But Ford adapted it for complex manufacturing, meticulously breaking down tasks into simple, repetitive steps. The result? The time it took to build a Model T chassis plummeted from 12 hours to a mind-boggling 93 minutes! This innovation didn't just slash production costs and boost output; it created a new paradigm for industrial efficiency, making the Model T affordable for the average American and setting the stage for modern mass production across countless industries. It was less about making a car and more about engineering the future of work itself, proving that sometimes, the biggest leaps come from simply moving things along.
