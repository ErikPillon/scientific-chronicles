Alfred Korzybski was a fascinating character – an engineer, philosopher, and rather intense polymath who decided humanity's biggest problems stemmed from how we use language and perceive reality. After witnessing the horrors of World War I, he became convinced that traditional modes of thought and communication were fatally flawed. So, he developed 'General Semantics,' a discipline aimed at improving human understanding and sanity by making our language and thinking more aligned with the non-verbal, ever-changing nature of reality. His most famous maxim, 'The map is not the territory,' encapsulates his core idea: our mental models and words are merely representations, not the reality itself, and mistaking one for the other leads to all sorts of logical blunders and conflicts. Korzybski wasn't just theorizing; he founded the Institute of General Semantics to teach people how to 'train their nervous systems' for more accurate evaluations. While often considered unconventional, his ideas profoundly influenced fields from psychology to communication studies, pushing us to be more critical of our assumptions and the words we use. He essentially offered a toolkit for upgrading our brains' operating system, to think and talk with more precision and less confusion.
