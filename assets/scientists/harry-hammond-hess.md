During World War II, Harry Hammond Hess was not just commanding a US Navy attack transport ship; he was also stealthily mapping the Pacific Ocean floor using the ship's sonar. Talk about multitasking! This American geologist-turned-naval-officer gathered reams of data, discovering vast flat-topped seamounts (guyots) and mid-ocean ridges. After the war, connecting these findings with later magnetic anomaly data, Hess formulated his revolutionary theory of 'seafloor spreading' in 1960. He proposed that new oceanic crust forms at mid-ocean ridges and spreads outwards, pushing continents along. This wasn't just a neat idea; it was a cornerstone of plate tectonics, explaining everything from continental drift to mountain building. Hess basically showed us that our planet's crust isn't static, it's a dynamic, ever-moving conveyor belt. Minds blown, Earth moved!
