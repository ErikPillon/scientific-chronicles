Alfred G. Gilman was an American pharmacologist who cracked open one of the biggest mysteries in cell communication, earning him a Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1994, which he shared with Martin Rodbell. Imagine a cellular phone network where messages need to get from the outside (like a hormone or a drug) to the inside, triggering a specific response. Gilman's groundbreaking research identified and characterized G-proteins (Guanine nucleotide-binding proteins) as key players in this intricate signaling relay. These little molecular switchboards sit inside cell membranes, acting as crucial intermediaries between external signals (like light, odors, or a vast array of hormones and neurotransmitters) and internal cellular responses. His discovery explained how many drugs work their magic and why some fail, providing fundamental insights into diseases like cholera, cancer, and heart disease. Essentially, Gilman gave us the owner's manual for a huge chunk of cell communication, paving the way for the development of countless new therapies. He was the guy who found the secret handshake cells use to talk to each other.
