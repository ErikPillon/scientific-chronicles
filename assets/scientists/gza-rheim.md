Géza Róheim was basically Sigmund Freud's most adventurous student. Instead of staying in the comfy confines of Viennese consulting rooms, this Hungarian psychoanalyst packed his bags and journeyed to remote corners of the world – like Central Australia and Melanesia – to study "primitive" cultures firsthand. He was a trailblazer in "psychoanalytic anthropology," arguing that cultural practices and myths could be understood through Freudian concepts like the Oedipus complex and childhood development. He literally lived with indigenous groups to gather data, a rarity for psychoanalysts then. His work, while sometimes controversial, pushed the boundaries of both psychoanalysis and anthropology, showing how the human psyche and societal structures might be intertwined in unexpected ways. He basically wanted to put the couch in the jungle, and he did.
