Meet Audrey Richards, a British social anthropologist who wasn't afraid to get her hands dirty (or dusty, more accurately) researching in colonial Africa. Born in 1899, she challenged the armchair anthropology of her time by conducting extensive fieldwork, particularly among the Bemba people of Northern Rhodesia (modern-day Zambia) in the 1930s. Her groundbreaking work focused on the daily lives of women, nutrition, and economic systems, rather than just kinship or politics. Her book "Land, Labour and Diet in Northern Rhodesia" (1939) was a landmark, showing how food and economic practices intertwined with social structures. She later became a key figure at Cambridge University, influencing generations of anthropologists. Richards basically told the academic world, "Hey, let's actually talk to people and see how they live!" making her a trailblazer in applied anthropology and a champion for understanding the often-overlooked practicalities of human societies.
