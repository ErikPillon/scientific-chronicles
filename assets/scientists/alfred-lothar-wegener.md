Alfred L. Wegener was a German polymath who dared to look at a world map and ask, 'Hey, do those continents look like they used to fit together?' And just like that, the groundbreaking, initially scorned, theory of continental drift was born. A meteorologist by trade, Wegener wasn't afraid to step outside his lane. He noticed the jigsaw puzzle fit of continents, the matching fossil records across oceans, and similar rock formations on separate landmasses. In 1912, he formally presented his idea that all Earth's continents were once jammed together in a supercontinent he dubbed 'Pangaea' and had slowly drifted apart over millions of years. This idea was absolutely revolutionary and, frankly, scoffed at by most geologists for decades because he couldn't explain *how* the continents moved. It took half a century and the discovery of seafloor spreading and plate tectonics to prove him right. Tragically, Wegener died on an expedition in Greenland before his theory gained widespread acceptance. He was a visionary who saw the big picture before anyone else, a true geological prophet whose ideas eventually moved mountains – or rather, explained how mountains (and continents) move.
