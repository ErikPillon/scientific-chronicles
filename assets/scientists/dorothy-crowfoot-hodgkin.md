Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin spent her career pointing X-rays at crystals and, through sheer patience and brilliant math, figuring out exactly how their atoms were arranged. That skill let her map the 3D structure of penicillin, vitamin B12, and eventually insulin, a molecule so fiendishly complex she chased it for over three decades. In 1964 she won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry, the third woman ever to do so, and remains the only British woman to win a Nobel in a science. Her hands were affected by rheumatoid arthritis for most of her adult life, which makes the steadiness of her crystallographic work even more remarkable. She also mentored a young chemistry student named Margaret Thatcher at Oxford, though the two ended up on rather different career paths. 🔬💊
