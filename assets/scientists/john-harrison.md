For centuries, sailors could measure latitude easily but had no reliable way to determine longitude at sea, a gap that caused countless shipwrecks and led the British government to offer a staggering cash prize for a solution. John Harrison, a working-class carpenter with no formal scientific training, spent over four decades building a series of increasingly refined marine chronometers, clocks precise enough to keep accurate time through storms, temperature swings, and the rolling of a ship, allowing navigators to calculate longitude by comparing local time to the clock's home-port time. His final design, the H4, was small enough to hold in one hand and accurate to within seconds over a months-long voyage. The scientific establishment, dominated by astronomers who favored a rival lunar-distance method, fought Harrison for years before he finally received his full prize money, in part after direct intervention from King George III. ⏱️🧭
