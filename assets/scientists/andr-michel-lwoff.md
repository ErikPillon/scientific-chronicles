André Lwoff, a French microbiologist with a keen eye for the invisible, cracked a fundamental code of life: how viruses, those tiny troublemakers, cleverly sneak their genetic material into bacterial DNA. This wasn't just some lab-bench curiosity; his groundbreaking work on lysogeny earned him a Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1965, shared with Jacques Monod and François Jacob. Lwoff's discoveries transformed our understanding of viral infections and gene regulation, laying crucial groundwork for modern molecular biology. He practically gave us the playbook for how some viruses turn their hosts into unwitting accomplices. A true wizard of the microbial world, he showed us that even the smallest life forms have the biggest secrets.
