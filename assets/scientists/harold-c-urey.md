Harold C. Urey was basically the OG of heavy water. He snagged a Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1934 for discovering deuterium, the heavier cousin of hydrogen. Not content with just that, he later became a big shot in the Manhattan Project, helping figure out how to separate uranium isotopes—because, you know, atomic bombs don't build themselves. After the war, he looked to the stars, becoming a leading light in astrochemistry and even pondered the origins of life on Earth, proposing theories on planetary formation. Urey was a big thinker who wasn't afraid to dive deep into both the subatomic and the cosmic.
