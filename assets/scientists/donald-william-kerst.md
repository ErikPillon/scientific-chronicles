In 1940, Donald Kerst built the first working betatron, a doughnut-shaped machine that uses a precisely shaped magnetic field to whip electrons around in circles, accelerating them to enormous energies without a huge linear track. It sounds simple in hindsight, but getting the magnetic geometry exactly right had stumped physicists for years. The betatron went on to become a workhorse for both nuclear physics research and, rather unglamorously, industrial X-ray inspection and cancer radiotherapy. Kerst kept tinkering with accelerator designs for decades afterward, including work on the ideas that eventually fed into modern particle colliders. Sometimes the most important machines in physics look, from the outside, like a metal bagel. 🥯⚛️
