Alfred Hershey was the quiet half of the dynamic duo (alongside Martha Chase) who, in 1952, performed one of the most elegant and impactful experiments in 20th-century biology. Picture this: scientists were scratching their heads, wondering if genes were made of DNA or protein. Both were complex molecules, but the general consensus leaned towards protein. Hershey, a bacteriophage expert, wasn't so sure. Along with Chase, he cooked up the now-famous Hershey-Chase experiment, using radioactive tracers to tag the DNA and protein of bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria). When these viruses infected bacteria, they found that the DNA was injected into the host cell, while most of the protein stayed outside. Bingo! It was the DNA carrying the genetic instructions. This experiment was the definitive proof that DNA, not protein, was the hereditary material, a revelation that cleared the path for Watson and Crick's double helix model and utterly transformed genetics. For this groundbreaking work, Hershey shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1969. He essentially gave DNA its rightful crown, confirming it was the true king of heredity.
