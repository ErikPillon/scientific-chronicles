Harry Harlow was an American psychologist who wasn't afraid to get up close and personal with monkeys to understand the mysteries of love. His groundbreaking, albeit ethically debated, experiments in the 1950s involved rhesus monkeys and two types of 'surrogate mothers': one made of wire, providing food, and another covered in soft terry cloth, but offering no sustenance. The results? The baby monkeys overwhelmingly preferred the comfy cloth mother, clinging to it for comfort and security, even when the wire mother was the sole food provider. Harlow's work fundamentally challenged the prevailing belief that attachment was solely based on nourishment, demonstrating the profound importance of 'contact comfort' and emotional bonds for psychological development. His findings reshaped theories on parenting and social development, proving that sometimes, a warm hug beats a full belly.
