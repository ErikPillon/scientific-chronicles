Ever wondered how continents drift or why the ocean floor looks like it's been neatly stitched together? Frederick Vine, along with his supervisor Drummond Matthews and Lawrence Morley, provided a huge piece of the puzzle. As a young PhD student in the 1960s, Vine helped formulate the Vine-Matthews-Morley hypothesis, proposing that the magnetic stripes observed on the ocean floor were a record of seafloor spreading. Essentially, as new oceanic crust forms at mid-ocean ridges, it records the Earth's magnetic field direction at that time, which periodically reverses. This creates a symmetrical pattern of magnetic anomalies on either side of the ridge. It was a brilliant, elegant solution that provided crucial evidence for Harry Hess's seafloor spreading concept and quickly became a cornerstone of the revolutionary theory of plate tectonics. Vine helped transform geology from a descriptive science to one underpinned by dynamic global processes, practically overnight!
