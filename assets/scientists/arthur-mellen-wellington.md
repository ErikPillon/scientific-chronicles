Ever wondered who made train travel both efficient and (relatively) affordable? Say hello to Arthur Mellen Wellington, the American civil engineer who put the "smart" in smart infrastructure, even before "smart" was a buzzword. Born in 1848, Wellington was a railway whiz who didn't just lay tracks; he laid down the economic principles that optimized railway design. His magnum opus, "The Economic Theory of the Location of Railways," became the bible for engineers, explaining how to choose routes not just for geographical ease, but for maximum economic benefit and efficiency. He basically invented cost-benefit analysis for railroads, ensuring that every curve and gradient wasn't just physically sound, but financially sensible. A prolific writer and editor, Wellington's sharp intellect and practical approach made him a towering figure in late 19th-century engineering. He showed that good engineering wasn't just about building things, but building them *right* and *smartly*, proving that sometimes, the best way to move forward is to think about the numbers first.
