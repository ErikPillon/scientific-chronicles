Benjamin Waterhouse, born in Newport, Rhode Island, in 1754, was the medical hero who basically saved early America from smallpox, one vaccination at a time. While Edward Jenner discovered the smallpox vaccine in England, it was Waterhouse, after studying in Europe, who brought it across the Atlantic. In 1800, he performed the first vaccinations in the U.S. on his own children, because, hey, what better way to prove it works than on your own kids, right? He then sent samples to President Thomas Jefferson, who became a huge advocate, helping to spread the practice throughout the country. Waterhouse faced skepticism and ridicule (as pioneers often do), but he persevered, publishing articles and giving lectures to convince the public and medical community of the vaccine's efficacy. He served as the first professor of theory and practice of medicine at Harvard Medical School and was a co-founder of the Massachusetts Medical Society. So, before you complain about getting your annual flu shot, spare a thought for Benjamin Waterhouse, who literally fought the good fight against one of history's deadliest diseases, ushering in an era of preventive medicine in the United States.
