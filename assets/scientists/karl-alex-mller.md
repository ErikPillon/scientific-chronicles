Karl Alex Müller was a true pioneer in the world of quantum materials, co-winning the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1987 for his groundbreaking discovery. Together with Johannes Georg Bednorz, he stumbled upon high-temperature superconductivity in a ceramic material – specifically, a lanthanum barium copper oxide. Before their work, superconductivity was thought to only happen at super-chilly temperatures near absolute zero, making it impractical for most applications. Müller's discovery cracked open a whole new realm, suggesting that materials could conduct electricity with zero resistance at far more accessible (though still very cold) temperatures. This wasn't just a neat lab trick; it set off a global race to find even 'warmer' superconductors and completely changed the game for materials science, promising everything from incredibly efficient power grids to levitating trains.
