Ever felt 'stressed out'? Thank (or blame) Hans Selye, the Austrian-Canadian endocrinologist who basically put the word 'stress' on the medical map. In the 1930s, while studying hormones, he noticed that animals subjected to various unpleasant stimuli (cold, surgical injury, toxic drugs) all reacted with a remarkably similar set of physiological changes – enlarged adrenal glands, shrunken lymph nodes, and stomach ulcers. He coined this the 'General Adaptation Syndrome' and was the first to rigorously define 'stress' as the body's non-specific response to any demand. Before Selye, 'stress' was mostly an engineering term referring to external pressure. He showed it was an internal biological response, revolutionizing our understanding of how our bodies react to pressure, good or bad. He even differentiated between 'eustress' (good stress) and 'distress' (bad stress). So, next time you're juggling deadlines, remember Selye, who taught us that sometimes the biggest challenge isn't the external pressure, but how our own bodies decide to throw an internal party in response.
