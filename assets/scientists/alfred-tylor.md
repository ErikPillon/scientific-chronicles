Alfred Tylor was a fascinating British scholar who dabbled successfully in both rocks and ancient humans. As a geologist, he was particularly interested in river terraces, those stair-like formations along river valleys that tell tales of past water levels and geological activity. He meticulously studied how these terraces formed and what they revealed about the Earth's history. But his curiosity didn't stop there. Tylor also ventured into anthropology, examining early human tools and artifacts found in these ancient riverbeds. He contributed to the understanding of the deep antiquity of humankind, connecting geological evidence with archaeological finds. While his brother, Edward Burnett Tylor, became more famous as a founding figure in anthropology, Alfred's contributions were solid, bridging the gap between Earth science and human history. He was a keen observer who understood that to really get a grip on human origins, you needed to understand the ground they walked on.
