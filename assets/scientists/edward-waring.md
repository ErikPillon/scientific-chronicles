Edward Waring was an 18th-century mathematical enigma, famous for being the Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge (a chair later held by Isaac Newton and Stephen Hawking) and for dropping a puzzle that stumped mathematicians for centuries. His most celebrated contribution is 'Waring's Problem,' a seemingly simple conjecture he threw into his 1770 book, *Meditationes Algebraicae*. It basically states that every natural number can be expressed as the sum of a fixed number of *k*-th powers. For example, every number is the sum of at most 4 squares, 9 cubes, or 19 fourth powers. He didn't prove it, just *proposed* it, sparking a mathematical firestorm that drove research in number theory for generations. Lagrange proved the four squares part, but the general case took much longer, finally solved by Hilbert in 1909. Waring was a quiet, almost reclusive genius, who, despite his introverted nature, managed to lay down a gauntlet that spurred some seriously heavy-duty mathematical thinking. He essentially said, 'Here's a problem, good luck!'
