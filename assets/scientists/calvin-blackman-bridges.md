Calvin Blackman Bridges was a true 'fly guy' in the best possible scientific sense! As a key member of Thomas Hunt Morgan's 'Fly Room' at Columbia University, Bridges became a master of Drosophila melanogaster (the common fruit fly). From 1912 until his untimely death in 1938, he meticulously studied their chromosomes, practically becoming a living encyclopedia of fly genetics. He developed the first detailed chromosome maps, providing crucial visual proof that genes really do sit in specific locations on chromosomes – a concept that was revolutionary at the time. His work on non-disjunction and sex determination was foundational. While Morgan got the Nobel, Bridges' tireless, detailed lab work provided the bedrock data. He basically wrote the book on how genes were physically organized, making him a silent giant in the early days of modern genetics.
