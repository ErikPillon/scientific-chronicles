Eugen Goldstein was a physicist who loved to play with high-voltage electricity and vacuum tubes, and good thing he did! This meticulous German experimentalist, in the late 19th century, made a discovery that profoundly influenced our understanding of atomic structure. While investigating cathode rays (streams of electrons), he noticed something peculiar: rays traveling in the opposite direction, passing through holes (canals) in the cathode. He dubbed them 'canal rays' or 'anode rays' in 1886. These turned out to be streams of positively charged ions, opening up a whole new world for subatomic particle research. Though often overshadowed by the electron's discovery, Goldstein's work was foundational, leading directly to J.J. Thomson's development of the mass spectrometer and Francis Aston's discovery of isotopes. Basically, he showed us that atoms weren't just negatively charged bits; they had a positive side too!
