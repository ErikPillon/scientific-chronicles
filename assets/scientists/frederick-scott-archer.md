Before digital cameras, before even easy film, there was the collodion process, and the genius behind it was Frederick Scott Archer. This British sculptor-turned-photographer wasn't happy with the fuzzy, slow daguerreotypes of his era. So, in 1851, he cooked up a method using wet collodion on glass plates, which produced incredibly sharp images with much shorter exposure times. Think instant selfies, but with a lot more chemical fumes and careful timing. The catch? The plates had to be prepared, exposed, and developed all while still wet, making it a bit of a logistical nightmare for landscape photographers. Despite its challenges, Archer's invention democratized photography, making high-quality images more accessible. Tragically, he never patented his process, dying in relative poverty, while others grew rich from his brilliant, if somewhat messy, innovation.
