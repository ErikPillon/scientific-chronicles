Douglas Houghton Campbell, born in 1859, was an American botanist who loved a good plant mystery, especially if it involved ferns. He was a Stanford University professor for ages, becoming a giant in the field of pteridology (that's the study of ferns, for those not fluent in botanical jargon). Campbell wasn't just collecting leaves; he was obsessively tracing the evolutionary relationships of ferns, mosses, and other ancient plant groups. His work on their embryology and morphology helped scientists understand how plants diversified over millions of years. He traveled the globe, from Java to Hawaii, collecting specimens and insights, practically writing the book on how these 'primitive' plants developed. Basically, he was figuring out who was related to whom in the plant kingdom long before DNA tests were a thing. He passed away in 1953, leaving behind a legacy that rooted our understanding of plant evolution much more firmly.
